CHESS GAME PRESENTATION INTENT
==============================

Purpose
-------

This document records the intended long-term visual presentation of the chess
game. It should be consulted before changing the chess board, piece rendering,
camera/view projection, battle UI, animations, or visual effects. Its purpose is
to keep short-term implementation decisions from creating roadblocks for the
eventual presentation.

This document concerns the chess battle itself, not the overworld.


Core Experience
---------------

The chess game should feel like an in-person game played across a physical
board, rather than an abstract online-chess interface. The player should feel as
though they are sitting at a table in the game world with their opponent.

Chess is also more than an ordinary tabletop game in this setting. It is a
somewhat divine act connected to the fundamental pillars of reality. The battle
presentation should therefore feel more immediate, detailed, and "real" than
the overworld while remaining recognizably part of the same pixel-art game.

The guiding tone is classic old-school Dungeons & Dragons and the early video
game RPGs it inspired.


Camera and Board
----------------

* The chess presentation will remain entirely 2D.
* The board should be tilted only slightly away from the viewer: use the minimum
  amount of foreshortening needed to make it believable that the player is
  seated at a table looking at a real board.
* Players should see the board from their own seat. The near and far sides must
  therefore be player-relative rather than permanently assigned to White or
  Black.
* The complete board should normally remain visible. Rare temporary zooms are
  acceptable for dramatic moments, but ordinary play must preserve a clear view
  of all 64 squares.
* Board readability takes precedence over physically exact perspective.
* Pieces should retain a consistent scale relative to one another within a
  battle view. Do not make distant pieces smaller merely to imitate real
  perspective. The complete piece set may scale uniformly with the board and
  window so the position remains as large and readable as the display allows.
* Taller pieces may obscure a modest portion of the square behind them. This is
  desirable when it reinforces the physical viewpoint, provided the board state
  and interaction markers remain quickly readable.
* The board should eventually read as a physical object, including an
  appropriate frame, edge, thickness, and contact with the surface beneath it.

The board view should keep logical chess coordinates separate from projected
screen geometry. Future implementations should be able to change the four
screen-space corners of the board, its tilt, scale, or player-relative
orientation without changing chess rules or controller logic. Square surfaces,
hit regions, highlights, piece anchors, and effect positions should all derive
from the same authoritative board projection.


Physical Setting
----------------

Battles will eventually occur in a variety of environments, and the surface and
surroundings should reflect where the encounter happens: grass, dirt, asphalt,
a tavern table, a glossy corporate table, or other locations in the game world.

The initial assumption can simply be a portable table. Chess players in the
region may carry folding tables with them. The chess board should not be coupled
to the current wood background or to any single permanent environment.


Pieces and Pixel-Art Standard
-----------------------------

The current working asset standard is:

* Pawns use a 32 x 32 pixel canvas.
* Taller pieces use a 32 x 64 pixel canvas.
* Pieces share a consistent width and should use a consistent bottom-center
  ground anchor where they contact the board.
* Special pieces should be normalized to the same presentation standard rather
  than corrected at runtime with arbitrary per-piece scale values.

This exact canvas standard is not irrevocably final, but all piece assets should
be authored against a deliberate common standard. The chess presentation may
feel analogous to "32-bit" art while the overworld feels more "16-bit": still
pixelated, but with greater detail and physical presence. Nearest-neighbor
sampling remains preferred, but fractional pixel scaling and its uneven pixel
dimensions are acceptable when they materially improve board readability.

Visual effects should probably be normalized around the same pixel-art scale
and presentation, although the exact standard for effects is not yet decided.
Do not make assumptions that would prevent effects from being revised later.


Hands, Movement, and Physical Behavior
--------------------------------------

The physical framing should eventually become quite literal:

* A character's hand moves an ordinary piece after the player selects a move.
* A hand removes an ordinarily captured piece from the board.
* Hands may set up all of the pieces during an opening animation.
* Player characters and NPCs can physically emote at the table. For example, an
  opponent may slam a fist down and slightly jostle the pieces.
* Pieces may gradually fall slightly out of alignment as a game progresses.
* An NPC may occasionally straighten their pieces before yielding the turn.

These details should create tactility and character without compromising pace
or making input feel sluggish. Move selection happens first; the corresponding
physical animation then expresses the chosen action.

King pieces are magical and are not moved by a character touching them.
King-generated summons, including Bone Pawns, may also move on their own. Some
abilities and board effects may destroy, remove, or displace pieces directly, so
the presentation system must not assume that every move or capture uses a hand.

Future animation code should distinguish at least ordinary hand-driven moves,
magical self-propelled moves, ordinary hand-removed captures, summons, and
ability-driven destruction or removal.


Interface and Gameplay Markers
------------------------------

The interface should be as diegetic as practical, but it does not have to obey
strict diegesis when clarity would suffer.

* Active-ability cooldowns may be expressed through the king itself, such as an
  aura, floating orbs, or another magical indicator.
* When an active ability is ready, selecting the king should show its normal
  legal moves plus a distinct ability option hovering within or immediately
  around the king.
* HP bars or similarly conventional UI may be used if no sufficiently clear
  diegetic treatment is available.
* Turn state, cooldown state, damage, stun, and other important conditions must
  remain immediately understandable.

Move and target markers may resemble the clear interaction language of online
chess platforms such as Chess.com or Lichess. They represent information in the
player character's mind rather than literal objects on the board, so they are
allowed to be clean, abstract overlays.

Different interactions should remain visually distinguishable where useful:
selected piece, legal movement, capture, active ability, reaction choice,
danger, and other special targets. Markers must remain legible when pieces
partially cover their squares.


Lighting and Effects
--------------------

Constant realistic shadows are not required and should not muddy the board.
Lighting may become more pronounced when an environment or dramatic moment
benefits from it. Examples include:

* Long atmospheric shadows during a game at sunset.
* Stark temporary piece shadows radiating away from a lightning strike.
* Magical illumination associated with kings, summons, or abilities.

Lighting and effects may temporarily heighten or bend reality, but ordinary
board state must remain readable before and after the effect.


Implementation Guardrails
-------------------------

When making chess-presentation changes:

* Preserve the separation between authoritative chess state and presentation.
* Do not encode perspective, physical animation, or asset-specific layout into
  the chess model.
* Do not assume that board cells are permanently axis-aligned rectangles.
* Derive visible square geometry, collision geometry, highlights, anchors, and
  effects from one board-projection system.
* Do not rely on per-piece runtime scale exceptions as the long-term asset
  normalization strategy.
* Anchor pieces by their physical contact point with the board.
* Ensure nearer rows render over farther rows while retaining explicit control
  of hands, effects, and exceptional magical actions.
* Keep the environment replaceable and avoid coupling board layout to a single
  background.
* Prefer physically immersive behavior, but deliberately break perspective or
  realism whenever necessary to preserve fast, reliable board comprehension.
* Avoid designing the presentation around the assumption that every action is
  a standard chess move performed by a hand.


Open Questions
--------------

The following details remain intentionally undecided:

* Whether 32 x 32 and 32 x 64 will remain the exact final piece canvas sizes.
* The precise common canvas and scale rules for visual effects.
* The final visual design of HP and other unavoidable status displays.
* The exact magical representation of active abilities and cooldowns.
* How environment-specific lighting and surroundings will be supplied to a
  battle scene.
* The eventual timing and frequency of hand animations, piece misalignment,
  piece-straightening behavior, and character emotes.

These questions should remain configurable rather than being answered through
hard-coded assumptions in foundational board code.
