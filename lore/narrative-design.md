# Narrative design

Status: Working

This page describes how to make the intended reactivity manageable. It is a
writing model, not yet a runtime specification.

The [writing framework](writing-framework.md) defines the big-to-small process.
The [core theme](themes/core.md) provides the primary question that this
reactivity should let the player answer through action.

## Thematic construction

- A location conflict poses a particular variation of a major thematic
  question.
- Important characters embody distinct answers, but remain people with needs,
  histories, contradictions, and positions on other axes.
- Quests place those answers under material pressure.
- Choices let the player enact an answer rather than merely select a slogan.
- Consequences reveal who benefited, who paid, and how the world interpreted
  the player's action.

No character should exist solely to represent a coordinate on a spectrum, and
no single spectrum should explain the whole character.

## Consequence model

Prefer a small number of authored states that produce visible differences over
tracking every action numerically.

| Layer | What it remembers | What the player should notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global | A few decisions or values that shape the final crisis | Changed allies, options, opposition, and endings |
| Location | The condition and leadership of a community | Changed scenery, services, residents, dialogue, and quests |
| Relationship | A character's attitude plus specific remembered acts | Different candor, loyalty, conflict, recruitment, or departure |
| Quest | How a concrete problem ended and who paid the cost | Follow-up dialogue and later opportunities or complications |
| Knowledge | What the player and individual NPCs have learned | Recognition, surprise, changing strategy, and new interpretations |

A relationship should not be reduced to one approval number when a specific
memory matters. “Trusts the player” and “knows the player lied about the mine”
can coexist.

## Branching rule

Branches may rejoin at major structural beats while their consequences persist.
The game does not need a separate campaign for every choice. A shared next
destination can contain different companions, community states, resources,
dialogue, and motives.

This creates reactivity without an exponentially growing plot tree.

## Standard for a major quest

A major quest should usually contain:

- Someone who wants something before the player arrives
- Pressure that prevents an easy resolution
- At least two defensible interpretations or priorities
- A use of chess that reveals character or culture, not merely a mandatory
  battle inserted between conversations
- A choice, approach, or performance that changes persistent state
- At least one later acknowledgment of the outcome
- A specific variation of a larger thematic question, even if the theme is
  never named in dialogue

Not every quest needs multiple endings. Smaller quests can deepen a person or
place, teach a rule, foreshadow a conflict, or make a prior consequence felt.

## Choice standard

A meaningful choice does not require evenly pleasant options, but it should
make the relevant values and likely immediate stakes legible. Whenever
possible, competing choices should arise from different defensible priorities
rather than a labeled benevolent answer and a gratuitously cruel answer.

Refusal, neglect, ignorance, and failure can also express an answer. They should
produce authored consequences when they matter instead of being treated as the
absence of story.

## Location outcomes

“Improved” and “ruined” should describe concrete lived conditions, not morality
labels. For each major location, define:

- What physically changes
- Who gains and loses power
- Who stays, leaves, arrives, or dies
- Which services, routes, or quests change
- How residents interpret the player's responsibility
- Whether an apparent improvement carries a later cost

## Endings

Build endings from a limited set of choices that express the game's central
question. Avoid one ending per permutation. A useful structure is:

1. A primary ending determined by the final stance or resolution.
2. Variations determined by major allies and global decisions.
3. Short epilogues for important companions and locations.

The primary ending should answer the central question at its greatest scale:
the player can oppose the occult machinery that treats the Kingdom as pieces,
or attempt to seize that machinery and become master of the board. Other major
themes, alliances, relationships, and location outcomes can complicate that
answer through variations and epilogues.
